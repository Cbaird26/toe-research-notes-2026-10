                                                     THE UNBALANCED GROUP THEOREM

                              Abstract. The classification of finite simple groups (CFSG) identifies the basic building blocks of all
                              finite groups. It grew from nineteenth-century work of Galois and Jordan into a collective effort involving
                              many mathematicians, with Gorenstein playing a central organizing role. Its proof is a vast network of
                              interdependent results such as the Feit–Thompson odd-order theorem, the signalizer functor theorem, and
                              Aschbacher’s classical involution theorem. The original papers make up the first-generation proof. The
                              second-generation treatment, initiated by Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon, reorganizes and simplifies much
                              of this work.
                                  A key structural result in the first-generation program is the Unbalanced Group Theorem (UGT). Under
                              the inductive hypotheses of the classification, it says that a nontrivial subgroup of odd order normal in the
                              centralizer of an involution forces the ambient simple group to be a group of Lie type in odd characteristic

                                                                            1. Overview
                          The classification of finite simple groups (CFSG) is a monumental achievement in the history of mathe-
                       matics. A group is simple if it is nontrivial and has no normal subgroups other than itself and the identity
                       subgroup. Every finite group can be broken into simple factors by a composition series. Indeed, the
                       Jordan–Hölder theorem says that the resulting factors, counted with multiplicity, do not depend on the
                       series. Classifying these factors does not tell us every way to assemble a finite group, but it tells us which
                       building blocks are available. The CFSG catalogs every finite simple group:
                       Theorem (Classification of finite simple groups). Every finite simple group is either cyclic of prime order,
                       an alternating group An for n ≥ 5, a simple group of Lie type, or one of the twenty-six sporadic groups,
                       named after their discoverers apart from the Baby Monster and the Monster.
                          The proof grew into a collective enterprise spanning much of the twentieth century. Richard Brauer
                       made centralizers of elements of order two central to the recognition of simple groups. Walter Feit and
                       John Thompson’s odd-order theorem, published in 1963, showed that every nonabelian finite simple group
                       has such an element [11]. Michio Suzuki and Thompson made powerful tools for recovering a group from
                       its subgroups, while Daniel Gorenstein gave the effort a guiding program and an organizing framework.
                       Michael Aschbacher, Richard Lyons, Ronald Solomon, and many others supplied the structural and recog-
                       nition theorems that carried that program forward. Solomon’s historical account [25] describes both the
                       ideas and the extraordinary collaboration behind them.
                          What is now referred to as the first-generation proof is made up of an extensive web of papers, whose
                       main framework was already established by the early 1980s. Gorenstein’s 1983 volume [16] records much
                       of the relevant work. Work toward completion and verification continued. For example, the substantial
                       remaining case of quasithin groups was resolved by Aschbacher and Stephen Smith in work published in
                       2004. Aschbacher’s account [3] explains the distinction between the early announcements and the work
                       needed to secure the proof. Beginning in the early 1980s, Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon also undertook

   CFSG should be thought of as a problem of identification. Suppose there were a finite simple group
of smallest order missing from the “known list”. All the simple groups arising as sections of its proper
subgroups would already be known. One then examines those subgroups, compares the information they
provide, and proves that the supposedly new group must itself be one of the known groups. To this end,
group theorists have made great use of centralizers of involutions: the subgroups consisting of all elements
that commute with a given element of order two. Such centralizers are proper subgroups of a nonabelian
simple group, but their structure can constrain the whole group.
   The Unbalanced Group Theorem (UGT) is one of the key results that makes this “local-to-global”
strategy work. Roughly speaking, if even one involution centralizer has a nontrivial normal subgroup of
odd order, then the ambient simple group belongs to particular familiar families.
   To make this precise, we recall some definitions. Throughout, all groups in this paper are finite. Let G
be a group. An involution is an element of order two. The centralizer of a subset A of G is
                               CG (A) = {g ∈ G : ga = ag for every a ∈ A}.
We denote this centralizer by CG (t) when A = {t}. The odd core O(G) = O2′ (G) is the largest normal
subgroup of G of odd order. It exists because the product of two normal subgroups of odd order is again
a normal subgroup of odd order. The subscript 2′ means “prime to two.”
  Normality depends on the ambient group. In particular, a subgroup can be normal in CG (t) without
being normal in G. Although a nonabelian simple group G has no nontrivial normal subgroup of odd
order, an involution centralizer CG (t) may have one. The unbalanced-group problem asks what this tells
us about G.
Problem 1.1 (Unbalanced-group problem: simple-group case). Which nonabelian finite simple groups G
contain an involution t for which O(CG (t)) ̸= 1?
   Such a simple group is called unbalanced. Generally, a finite group G is unbalanced if O(CG (t)) ≰ O(G)
for some involution t ∈ G. For a nonabelian simple group G, the Feit–Thompson theorem [11] implies G
has even order and hence O(G) = 1, so the two conditions agree.
   The Unbalanced Group Theorem answers this question under the inductive hypothesis used in the
classification program, which we now make precise. A section of a group X is a quotient A/N , where
A ≤ X and N ⊴ A. Let K be the catalog of known nonabelian finite simple groups. Namely, they are
the alternating groups, the simple groups of Lie type, and the twenty-six sporadic groups. A K-group is
a group whose nonabelian simple sections belong to this catalog. A group is K-proper if every proper
subgroup is a K-group. This condition is natural in a classification proof. If G is a nonabelian simple
group of least order outside the catalog, every nonabelian simple section of a proper subgroup has smaller
order. Consequently G is K-proper.
   Groups of Lie type are the finite groups constructed from algebraic groups over finite fields, together
with their twisted analogs. The groups PSLn (q) are examples; they are the determinant-one matrices
over the finite field of q elements modulo the scalar matrices. Similarly, PGLn (q) is the group of all
invertible matrices modulo the nonzero scalar matrices. We say that a simple group is of odd Lie type if
it is isomorphic to a simple group of Lie type in odd characteristic. There are small coincidences such as
A5 ∼= PSL2 (5). We now state the simple-group case of the Unbalanced Group Theorem.
Theorem 1.2 (UGT). Let G be a nonabelian simple K-proper group. If O(CG (t)) ̸= 1 for some involution
t ∈ G, then either G is of odd Lie type, or G ∼
                                              = A4a+3 for some positive integer a.

UGT therefore removes all unbalanced groups from the remaining classification problem.
   The theorem also has an important connection with the B-theorem. Roughly speaking, the B-theorem
ensures that the simple pieces visible after removing the odd core of an involution centralizer can be
realized by suitable subgroups of the centralizer itself. More precisely, when O(G) = 1, every quasisimple
component of CG (t)/O(CG (t)) is the image of a quasisimple component of CG (t). We define components in
Section 2. This lifting property allows one to study actual subgroups of G, compare them across different
centralizers, and apply recognition theorems to identify G.
   For a smallest counterexample to CFSG, the displayed consequence of UGT makes this lifting property
immediate. Namely, there is no odd core to remove. The corresponding B-property for its proper subgroups
and their sections follows from independently established results for K-groups. Therefore, the simple-group
form of UGT supplies the ambient reduction needed here without requiring an independent proof of the
unrestricted B-theorem as an additional input. For the historical relationship between the two theorems,
see [13, §1], [16, Chapter 3], and [5, §§1.7–1.8].
   The restriction to involutions in G distinguishes our statement from some other formulations of UGT.
Remark 1.4. More general versions of UGT treat involutions acting from outside a simple normal sub-
group. Theorem 1.2 is the simple-group form required for the classification induction.

1.1. An obstruction to an accepted route to UGT. Our work on formalizing CFSG requires making
the hypotheses and dependencies of its many theorems explicit. In this effort, we have assembled a
comprehensive errata list. Most of the errors are straightforward to correct: typographical errors, omitted
hypotheses, and similar local issues. The difficulty considered here requires a change to an argument.
   The first-generation proof of UGT is not contained in a single paper. Gilman–Solomon’s Theorem A
[13, p. 59] proves the Unbalanced Group Conjecture for a group whose proper sections satisfy it, subject to
Hypothesis S [13, p. 58], a list of standard-component problems, each asking that a group with a standard
component of a specified type be identified. These problems were settled in separate papers over the
following years, with the PSL3 (4) case completed by Cheng and Held in 1985 [10, pp. 147–148]. No single
reference, as far as we know, records the point at which Hypothesis S was fully settled. Gorenstein’s 1983
volume [16] develops the unbalanced-group argument; the historical overview in [17, pp. 40–41] describes
the collective work on the related B2 -property. The pattern, a result announced modulo components
completed later, is discussed in [3]. Walter’s 1986 memoir [27] gives a treatment organized around the
signalizer functor method, and is recognized by Aschbacher [4, §4, p. 10] as a route to UGT and the
B-conjecture. It is the treatment examined here.

   Unexpectedly, our formalization found explicit counterexamples to a closure assertion in Walter’s proof
of UGT [27], leaving a key step of that argument unjustified. We explain the obstruction and develop an
alternative argument for the simple-group case using Solomon’s 1976 theory of maximal 2-components.
Remark 1.6 (Other routes to UGT). This difficulty does not obstruct CFSG. In particular, we have
Gorenstein’s account following an argument of Morton Harris [16, §4.5, p. 291]. There is also a proof of
UGT through the second-generation work of Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon (GLS). Under the inductive
hypothesis of Theorem 1.2, the nontrivial odd-order normal subgroup places the simple group in GLS’s
odd-type case. Their classification of that case [20, Part III, Chapter 12, Theorem 1.17] identifies it as
a known group. Namely, the known-group analysis [19, Theorem 7.7.1], with these corrections [21], then
gives the required UGT family restriction. This is a short deduction from substantial second-generation
input, not a repair of the obstruction described below. A shorter deduction from the reductions used in
this paper is recorded in Theorem 9.5.
   We outline the proof route where the difficulty arises. The starting point is a nonabelian simple K-proper
group G with an involution t such that O(CG (t)) ̸= 1. The goal is to show that G is in one of the families
in Theorem 1.2. The reductions either identify G with one of the required groups or lead to a configuration
involving two commuting involutions and a 2-component of one of their centralizers. A 2-component is a
perfect subnormal subgroup whose quotient by its odd core is quasisimple; after further quotienting by the
center, one obtains a nonabelian simple group (see Section 2).
   The task is to find a 2-component whose structure and position in G allow a recognition theorem to
identify G. To do this, the proof compares 2-components in different involution centralizers and selects
one that is maximal with respect to these comparisons. Results of Thompson–Burgoyne and Aschbacher,
together with the alternating-group analyses, handle many of the possibilities. The remaining cases require
the work of Foote, Gilman–Solomon, Griess–Solomon, and Harris [2, 5, 9, 12, 13, 22, 23].
   The difficulty arises in the comparison stage. The argument works with components whose associated
simple quotients belong to a specified family. A later step requires a quotient obtained by taking an
involution centralizer to remain in that family. The closure assertion used to justify this can fail. Namely,
an involutory automorphism can have a centralizer with a component whose simple quotient lies outside the
chosen family. Therefore, the inference placing the resulting component back in the family is unjustified.
   This assertion is in John Walter’s 1986 memoir [27], in the preparation for Part II, Proposition 5.9(ii).
The proposition appears on p. 145, with its proof on pp. 145–148. The passage on p. 136 following (5.2)
introduces the family L0 consisting of the groups PSL2 (q) for odd prime powers q other than 9, Fermat

primes, and Mersenne primes. A Fermat prime has the form 22 + 1 for an integer n ≥ 0, and a Mersenne
prime has the form 2n − 1 for an integer n ≥ 2.
   The obstruction comes from field involutions, automorphisms induced by the order-two automorphism
of a quadratic extension of finite fields. For PSL2 (r2 ), this automorphism raises each matrix entry to its
rth power. The following proposition shows how a group in L0 can have a field-involution centralizer whose
component lies outside L0 .

   We explain where the closure assertion enters Walter’s proof. The argument compares components in
different involution centralizers and needs to show that a newly encountered component still belongs to a
specified family. The type of a component is the isomorphism type of its nonabelian simple quotient, after
removing its odd core and center. Knowing that two components have the same type transfers membership
in a family only if one already knows that this common type belongs to that family.
   On p. 146 of [27], the relevant family is denoted by M2 and consists of components of L0 -type. Starting
from a component Lt , Walter forms
                                                 Lt,s = L(CLt (s)),
the layer of the centralizer of the involution s in Lt , using his notation for the layer. In the branch Mst ̸= Ms ,
that is, when conjugation by t sends Ms to a different component, the proof identifies the type of Ms with
that of Lt,s and uses this identification to place Ms in M2 .
   The missing justification is the assertion that the type of Lt,s still belongs to L0 . Membership of the
starting type in L0 does not establish this. Indeed, the calculation above shows that taking an involution
centralizer can produce an excluded type. Equality of the types of Ms and Lt,s cannot supply the missing
membership. For instance, if the resulting type is PSL2 (7), identifying another component with that type
does not put either one back in L0 . The subsequent argument within M2 therefore requires an additional
justification or a different comparison argument.
   The scope of these counterexamples deserves emphasis.
Remark 1.8. The examples above refute the closure assertion used in the proof of Proposition II.5.9(ii).
They do not provide groups that violate its conclusion. Namely, we have identified a gap in this inference,
not a counterexample to the proposition itself or to UGT.
   Here we give an alternative argument. Rather than assuming that the list L0 survives every comparison,
it keeps track of the actual components and the involutions used to compare them. Under suitable max-
imality and rank hypotheses, we prove that the relevant comparisons preserve the simple quotient. The
precise statement is Theorem 2.3 below, which is the main theorem of this paper. Solomon’s theory of
maximal 2-components [26] supplies the method for this comparison argument. The maximality reductions
come from Gilman–Solomon [13], and the final recognition uses the work of Foote and Harris as described in
Section 9. We develop these arguments with the witnessing involutions and the passages through odd-core
quotients made explicit.

strategy. For a systematic exposition of component calculus and known-group calculations, we also cite the

2.2. Component calculus. The following facts govern passage to odd-core quotients and to centralizers
of 2-subgroups. We use the component calculus as presented by Gorenstein, Lyons, and Solomon [18, §5],
together with the coprime-action results in Gorenstein’s Finite Groups [15, Chapter 5]. If R = O(X),
the quotient map gives a bijection between the 2-components of X and the ordinary components of X/R.
The inverse image of a component has a unique 2-component, namely its perfect residual. We call this
subgroup its canonical perfect lift. A subnormal subgroup remains subnormal after intersection with
another subgroup, and the image of a 2-component under a surjective homomorphism with odd kernel
is a 2-component.
   We also use L2′ -balance: if a 2-group U acts on X, then L2′ (CX (U )) ≤ L2′ (X). In its comparison
form, if a, b are commuting involutions and J is a 2-component of CG (a) with b ∈ CG (J), then there is
a 2-component K of CG (b) such that Jb ≤ ⟨K, K a ⟩. See [18, Theorem 5.24] and [13, p. 56]. Thus, the
comparison relation in Theorem 2.1 always has a target for these witnesses.
   An action of U on R is coprime if |U | and |R| are relatively prime. For such an action, we use R =
[R, U ]CR (U ) and [R, U, U ] = [R, U ]. If U is a fourgroup and R has odd order, then

                                      9. The Deduction of UGT
   The deduction of UGT combines Foote’s recognition theorem [12] with the reductions of Gilman–Solomon
[13, §§1, 3–4], including the recognition result of Foote and Harris recorded in their Theorem 1.7. The
comparison theorem supplies the passage from pointed maximality to the maximality required for recog-
nition. We first specify the maximality and core conditions used in this application. Call a pair (x, L)
Foote-maximal if L is a 2-component of CG (x) and every chain of the unpointed relation generated by (2)
preserves τ (L). In the unpointed relation the witnessing involution may be chosen anew at each step. Such
a pair is pointed-maximal, since every pointed chain is also an unpointed chain.
   If m2 (CG (L)) = 1, then (x, L) is Foote-maximal: the distinct commuting witnesses of any first step
would generate a fourgroup in CG (L).
   An unbalancing triple (x, y, L) consists of commuting involutions x, y ∈ G and a 2-component L of CG (x)
such that
                               [L, y] = L = [L, D],      D = O(CG (y)) ∩ CG (x).
These equalities imply that y normalizes L and x ̸= y.
   For a finite group H, let C(H) denote the following hereditary core condition. For every subgroup U ≤ H,
every pair of commuting involutions a, b ∈ U , and every 2-component Y of CU (b), either τ (Y ) ∼ = PSL2 (q)
for an odd prime power q, or
                                       (O(CU (a)) ∩ CU (b))′ , Y ≤ O(CU (b)).
                                                               

Here U = H is allowed.
   Once its rank hypothesis is verified, the comparison theorem supplies the maximality needed for Foote’s
recognition theorem. The alternatives requiring further attention are an action kernel of 2-rank one and
four explicit exceptional pairs.

Here F ∗ (G) = G and Z(G) = 1. This formulation includes odd-core quotients isomorphic to 2A7 ; it does
not require the quotient to be SL2 (q).
